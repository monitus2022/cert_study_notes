# 7. Finetuning

---

## What Finetuning Is

- Finetuning adapts a model to a specific task by further training it.
- It adjusts the model's weights, unlike prompt-based methods (Chapters 5 and 6).
- Prompt-based methods adapt via instructions, context, and tools instead.
- Finetuning can train the whole model or only part of it.

---

## What Finetuning Can Improve

- Domain-specific capabilities, such as coding or medical question answering.
- Model safety can also be strengthened.
- Most often used to improve instruction-following ability.
- Particularly to ensure adherence to specific output styles and formats.

---

## Costs and Trade-offs

- Finetuning requires more up-front investment than prompt-based methods.
- It incurs a much higher memory footprint.
- Naive finetuning often needs more memory than a single GPU offers.
- Reducing memory requirements motivates many finetuning techniques, including PEFT.

---

## Finetuning Basics
- Finetuning starts with a base model having some, but not all, needed capabilities.
- Goal: make the model perform well enough for a specific task.
- Finetuning is one form of transfer learning, introduced by Bozinovski and Fulgosi in 1976.
- Transfer learning moves knowledge from one task to accelerate learning on a related task.

---

## Transfer Learning and Sample Efficiency
- LLM pre-training on abundant text-completion data transfers to specialized tasks like legal QA or text-to-SQL.
- Transfer learning improves sample efficiency, letting a model learn behavior with fewer examples.
- Finetuning a good base model may need a few hundred examples; training from scratch may need millions.
- InstructGPT frames finetuning as unlocking capabilities already present but hard to access via prompting.

---

## Forms of Finetuning
- Finetuning extends pre-training and can take many forms.
- Self-supervised finetuning, or continued pre-training, uses cheap task-related data before expensive annotated data.
- Supervised finetuning uses input-output pairs; preference finetuning uses instruction, winning response, losing response.
- Infilling finetuning helps text editing and code debugging; long-context finetuning modifies positional embeddings.

---

## Who Does the Finetuning
- Finetuning is done by both model developers and application developers.
- Model developers typically post-train a model with finetuning techniques before releasing it.
- Developers may release several model versions, each finetuned to a different extent.
- Application developers can then choose the version that works best for them.

---

## Choosing Your Starting Model
- As an application developer, you might finetune a pre-trained model.
- More likely, you'll finetune a model that has already been post-trained.
- The more refined the model and the more relevant its knowledge, the less adaptation work.
- Model developers release variants made with different finetuning techniques, such as the Code Llama models.

---

## When Finetuning Is the Right Option
- Consider whether finetuning is right for you before choosing a technique.
- Compared to prompt-based methods, finetuning requires significantly more resources.
- Those resources include data, hardware, and ML talent, not just compute.
- Weigh these costs against prompt-based approaches before committing.

---

## Reasons to Finetune

- Finetuning is generally attempted after extensive prompt-based experiments, but the two approaches are not mutually exclusive.
- Real-world problems often require combining finetuning with prompting rather than choosing one.
- The primary reason is improving model quality: both general and task-specific capabilities.
- Finetuning commonly improves a model's ability to follow specific output structures, such as JSON or YAML.

---

## Adapting to Your Task and Reducing Bias

- A model strong on broad benchmarks may still underperform on your specific task.
- Finetuning with your data helps when the model wasn't sufficiently trained on your task.
- Example: an out-of-the-box model may handle standard SQL but fail on less common dialects or customer-specific queries.
- Finetuning can mitigate bias: curated data can counteract biases from the base model's training data.

---

## Bias Mitigation and Smaller Models

- Example: if a model assigns CEOs male-sounding names, finetuning on many female CEOs can mitigate this.
- Garimella et al. (2022): finetuning BERT-like models on text by women reduced gender biases; text by African authors reduced racial biases.
- Finetuning smaller models is more common: less memory, easier to finetune, cheaper and faster in production.
- Distillation finetunes a small model to imitate a larger model using data generated by it.

---

## Reasons Not to Finetune
- Many finetuning gains—performance, structured outputs—can also come from careful prompting and context.
- Finetuning for one task can improve it but degrade performance on other tasks.
- If tasks conflict, finetune on all queries, use separate models, or merge them.
- For new projects, finetuning is rarely the first thing to attempt.

---

## Costs and Maintenance
- Finetuning requires high up-front investment and continual maintenance.
- Annotated data can be slow and expensive; open source and AI-generated data vary in effectiveness.
- You need training knowledge: choosing base models, tuning knobs, monitoring, and debugging.
- You must plan serving, monitoring, and updating—and decide when a new base model justifies switching.

---

## Start with Prompting
- Start with prompting best practices; explore advanced solutions only if prompting proves inadequate.
- Test prompts thoroughly, since performance varies greatly across prompts.
- Many finetuning requests stem from minimal, unsystematic prompt experiments with unclear instructions.
- Prompt experiments build evaluation pipelines and annotation practices that are stepping stones for finetuning.

---

## Domain-Specific Tasks
- Don't assume general-purpose models fail at domain-specific tasks and require finetuning.
- BloombergGPT (50B parameters) cost an estimated $1.3M–$2.6M in compute, excluding data.
- GPT-4-0314 significantly outperformed BloombergGPT on financial benchmarks.
- Benchmarks can't capture real-world performance; BloombergGPT may still suit Bloomberg's use cases.

---

## RAG vs Finetuning: Diagnose Failures
- After maximizing prompting, choose RAG or finetuning based on failure type.
- Information-based failures: missing or outdated information; RAG supplies relevant sources.
- Behavioral issues: irrelevant outputs or wrong format; finetuning can help.
- Finetuning is for form, RAG is for facts; if both issues, start with RAG.

---

## Evidence: RAG Often Wins on Facts
- For current events, RAG outperformed finetuned models in Ovadia et al. (2024).
- RAG with base model outperformed RAG with finetuned models.
- Finetuning may improve one task but hurt performance in other areas.
- On MMLU, RAG outperformed finetuning for almost all question categories across three models.

---

## Workflow: Start with Prompting and RAG
- Define evaluation criteria and pipeline before adaptation; evaluate at every step.
- Try prompting, then add examples; use 1–50 examples depending on use case.
- For missing information, start RAG with basic term-based retrieval like BM25.
- Persistent information failures: advanced RAG; persistent behavioral issues: finetuning; both can combine.

---

## Memory Bottlenecks: Why Finetuning Is Memory-Intensive

- Finetuning is memory-intensive; many techniques aim to minimize memory footprint.
- Memory for finetuning is typically much higher than for inference.
- Understanding the bottleneck helps you select the best finetuning method.
- Back-of-the-napkin formulas help estimate hardware needed to serve or finetune a model.

---

## Key Contributors to Memory Footprint

- Three key contributors: number of parameters, number of trainable parameters, numerical representations.
- More trainable parameters means a higher memory footprint.
- Reducing trainable parameters lowers memory requirements for finetuning.
- This reduction is the motivation behind PEFT, parameter-efficient finetuning.

---

## Quantization Reduces Memory Footprint

- Quantization converts a model from more bits to fewer bits per value.
- It is a straightforward and efficient way to reduce memory footprint.
- A 13B-parameter model in FP32 uses 4 bytes per weight: 52 GB total.
- Reducing each value to 2 bytes cuts weight memory to 26 GB.

---

## Backpropagation and Trainable Parameters

- Trainable parameters can be updated during finetuning; frozen parameters are kept unchanged.
- Pre-training updates all parameters; inference updates none; finetuning may update some or all.
- Memory footprint during finetuning depends on the number of trainable parameters.
- Backpropagation training steps have two phases: forward pass and backward pass.

---

## Forward and Backward Pass

- Forward pass computes the output from the input; inference runs only this pass.
- Backward pass updates weights using aggregated signals from the forward pass.
- Training runs both passes; the loss is the gap between computed and expected output.
- The gradient measures how much each trainable parameter contributes to the mistake.

---

## Gradients and Optimizers

- Gradients are the derivative of the loss with respect to each trainable parameter.
- There is one gradient value per trainable parameter.
- A high gradient means the parameter contributes significantly and should be adjusted more.
- The optimizer decides readjustment; SGD and Adam are common, with Adam dominant for transformers.

---

## Memory Math: Why It Matters
- Model memory footprint depends on model size, workload, and optimization techniques.
- Knowing memory needs helps you pick the right hardware or check feasibility.
- A model needing 30 GB inference won't fit on a 24 GB chip.
- Inference and training have distinct memory profiles, driving separate chip designs.

---

## Memory Needed for Inference
- Only the forward pass runs; memory is needed for weights and activations.
- Weights memory: N × M, where N is parameter count and M is bytes per parameter.
- Activation and key-value vectors often assumed ~20% of weights memory.
- Total inference footprint: N × M × 1.2.
- Example: 13B params × 2 bytes = 26 GB weights; × 1.2 = 31.2 GB total.

---

## Memory Needed for Training
- Training memory = weights + activations + gradients + optimizer states.
- Each trainable parameter needs one gradient value plus 0–2 optimizer-state values.
- SGD: no state; momentum: one value; Adam: two values per parameter.
- Example: 13B params with Adam, 2 bytes each: 13B × 3 × 2 = 78 GB.
- Activation memory can dwarf weights; gradient checkpointing recomputes to save memory but slows training.

---

## Numerical Representations
- Values in neural networks are traditionally represented as float numbers, following IEEE 754.
- FP32 uses 32 bits (4 bytes) and is called single precision.
- FP64 uses 64 bits (8 bytes) and is called double precision; FP16 uses 16 bits (2 bytes), half precision.
- FP64 is rarely used in neural networks because of its memory footprint; FP32 and FP16 are more common.

---

## Float and Integer Formats
- Other popular AI float formats: BF16 (BFloat16), designed by Google for TPUs, and TF32 (TensorFloat-32), designed by NVIDIA for GPUs.
- Integer representations are becoming increasingly popular; common formats are INT8 and INT4.
- Each float format usually has 1 sign bit; the rest split between range and precision.
- More range bits widen the values representable; more precision bits make numbers more precise.

---

## Precision Reduction and Its Effects
- Formats with more bits are higher precision; converting to a lower-precision format means reducing precision.
- Reducing precision can cause a value to change or result in errors.
- BF16 and FP16 have the same number of bits, but BF16 has more range bits and fewer precision bits.
- Values out of bound in FP16 are rounded to infinity.

---

## Quantization: Basics

- Quantization reduces precision, shrinking a model's memory footprint cheaply and effectively.
- A 10B model needs 40 GB at 32-bit, only 20 GB at 16-bit.
- Low precision means any format with fewer bits than standard FP32.
- Strictly, quantization targets integers; in practice it covers all precision reduction.

---

## What and When to Quantize

- Weights and activations dominate inference memory; weight quantization is more common.
- Weight quantization causes less accuracy loss than activation quantization.
- Post-training quantization (PTQ) happens after full training and is by far the most common.
- PTQ is most relevant to application developers who don't train models.

---

## Inference Quantization in Practice

- Serving in 16 bits and lower has become standard since the late 2010s.
- LLM.int8() quantizes LLMs to 8 bits; QLoRA to 4 bits.
- Mixed precision reduces values when possible, keeps higher precision when necessary.
- Below 8 bits, values use minifloat formats (FP8, FP4) or integers (INT8, INT4).

---

## Limits and Trade-offs

- You cannot go below 1 bit per value; 1-bit attempts include BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, BitNet.
- BitNet b1.58 needs 1.58 bits per parameter, comparable to 16-bit Llama 2 up to 3.9B.
- Reduced precision enables larger batches and faster computation, lowering latency.
- Downsides: small value changes accumulate, and out-of-range values may become infinity.

---

## Training Quantization

- Two goals: good low-precision inference quality, and reduced training time and cost.
- QAT simulates low-precision behavior during training but doesn't cut training time.
- Training directly in low precision can achieve both goals.
- Character.AI trained entirely in INT8, removing the training/serving precision mismatch.

---

## Mixed Precision Training
- Mixed precision keeps a higher-precision copy of weights.
- Gradients and activations are kept in lower precision.
- Less-sensitive weights can use lower precision; sensitive weights use higher precision.
- LLM-QAT quantizes weights and activations to 4 bits, embeddings to 16 bits.

---

## Automatic Mixed Precision
- AMP functionality is offered by many ML frameworks.
- AMP can automatically set which model portions use lower precision.
- Different training phases can use different precision levels.
- Models may be trained in higher precision but finetuned in lower precision.

---

## Precision and Compute Access
- Foundation models are often trained in higher precision by well-resourced organizations.
- Developers with less compute access can finetune published models in lower precision.
- Finetuning large-scale models is memory-intensive.
- Lower-precision finetuning helps address memory constraints.

---

## Memory-Efficient Finetuning
- Techniques that reduce a model’s memory footprint make finetuning more accessible.
- This allows more people to adapt models to their applications.
- This section focuses on memory-efficient finetuning techniques.
- Memory-efficient finetuning centers around parameter-efficient finetuning.

---

## Model Merging
- Model merging is an exciting but more experimental approach to creating custom models.
- It is generally not considered finetuning, but it is complementary to finetuning.
- Finetuning tailors one model to specific needs.
- Model merging combines multiple models, often finetuned models, for the same purpose.

---

## Full Finetuning Basics
- Full finetuning trains all model parameters; trainable parameters equal total parameters.
- It resembles training but starts from previously trained weights, not randomized weights.
- More trainable parameters require more memory.

---

## Memory Math for a 7B Model
- FP16 model weights alone require 14 GB of memory.
- Adam optimizer in 16-bit adds 7B × 3 × 2 bytes = 42 GB.
- Total weights, gradients, and optimizer states: 14 GB + 42 GB = 56 GB.
- 56 GB exceeds most consumer GPUs (12–24 GB; higher-end up to 48 GB), before activations.

---

## Partial Finetuning and Its Limits

- Partial finetuning updates only some parameters, e.g., freezing nine of ten layers.
- This reduces trainable parameters to about 10% of full finetuning.
- It is parameter-inefficient: BERT large needed ~25% of parameters to match full finetuning on GLUE.
- Full finetuning also demands large amounts of high-quality annotated data most people can't afford.

---

## Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

- PEFT, introduced by Houlsby et al. (2019), inserts extra parameters in the right places.
- Two adapter modules were inserted into each transformer block of a BERT model.
- Original parameters stay frozen; only adapters are updated during finetuning.
- Result: within 0.4% of full finetuning using only 3% of trainable parameters on GLUE.

---

## Trade-offs and Benefits of PEFT

- Downside: adapters add layers and computational steps, increasing inference latency.
- PEFT enables finetuning on more affordable hardware, accessible to many more developers.
- PEFT methods are generally sample-efficient, sometimes working with just a few thousand examples.
- No clear threshold exists; parameter-efficient generally means orders of magnitude fewer trainable parameters.

---

## PEFT Techniques: Two Buckets
- PEFT methods generally fall into adapter-based and soft prompt-based buckets.
- Adapter-based methods add parameters, also called additive methods.
- LoRA is the most popular adapter-based method as of writing.
- Other adapter methods include BitFit, IA3, and LongLoRA.

---

## Soft Prompt-Based Methods
- Soft prompts introduce special trainable tokens fed alongside input tokens.
- Hard prompts are human-readable discrete tokens; soft prompts are continuous vectors.
- Hard prompts are static; soft prompts are optimized via backpropagation.
- Prefix-tuning, P-Tuning, and prompt tuning differ by insertion location.

---

## Popularity of PEFT Methods
- Analysis of over 1,000 GitHub issues in huggingface/peft (October 2024).
- LoRA dominates usage; soft prompts are less common but growing.
- Soft prompts attract those wanting customization beyond prompt engineering without full finetuning.
- Figure 7-10 shows issue counts as a popularity proxy.

---

## LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

- LoRA adds parameters without extra inference latency, unlike the original adapter method.
- It uses modules that can be merged back into the original layers.
- LoRA can be applied to individual weight matrices.
- A weight matrix is decomposed into two smaller matrices, updated, then merged back.

---

## How LoRA Works

- For weight matrix W of dimension n × m, choose rank r.
- Construct A (n × r) and B (r × m); their product is W_AB.
- Add W_AB to W to create W′, using hyperparameter ɑ to control contribution.
- During finetuning, only parameters in A and B are updated; W stays intact.

---

## LoRA Weight Decomposition

- LoRA applies to a weight matrix W.
- W is decomposed into the product of two matrices, A and B.
- During finetuning, only A and B are updated.
- W is kept intact.

---

## LoRA: Low-Rank Factorization Basics

- LoRA factorizes a large weight matrix into the product of two smaller matrices, cutting parameters.
- Example: a 9×9 matrix (81 parameters) becomes 9×1 and 1×9 matrices (18 parameters).
- The factorization rank equals the inner dimension; higher rank preserves more of the original matrix.
- Factorization is lossy—it only approximates the full-rank original matrix.

---

## LoRA Efficiency and Results

- Like adapters, LoRA is parameter-efficient and sample-efficient, using even fewer trainable parameters.
- For GPT-3, LoRA matched or beat full finetuning on several tasks with ~4.7M trainable parameters.
- That is 0.0027% of the parameters required by full finetuning.
- Low-rank factorization reduces both computation and memory requirements.

---

## Why Parameter Efficiency Works

- LLMs have many parameters but very low intrinsic dimensions, per Li, Aghajanyan, and Hu.
- Pre-training implicitly minimizes the model's intrinsic dimension; larger models tend to have lower intrinsic dimensions.
- Pre-training acts as a compression framework: better-trained models are easier to finetune with few parameters and little data.
- Low-rank pre-training has been tried (Sainath 2013, Povey 2018, Jaderberg 2014); SqueezeNet used 50× fewer parameters.

---

## Low-Rank Pre-Training Prospects

- Recent low-rank LLM efforts include ReLoRA (up to 1.3B parameters) and GaLore (comparable at 1B, promising at 7B).
- Scaling low-rank pre-training to hundreds of billions of parameters remains an open goal.
- If pre-training compresses intrinsic dimension, full-rank pre-training may still be necessary first.
- Open question: how much full-rank training is needed before switching to low-rank training.

---

## LoRA Configurations: Choosing Weight Matrices

- LoRA applies to individual weight matrices; efficiency depends on model architecture.
- Primarily used for transformers, especially attention module matrices: query, key, value, output projection.
- LoRA is typically applied uniformly to all matrices of the same type in a model.
- Naively, apply LoRA to all attention matrices, but hardware memory constrains trainable parameters.

---

## LoRA Under a Fixed Parameter Budget

- With limited memory, you can accommodate only a fixed number of trainable parameters.
- Question: given a fixed budget, which matrices maximize performance?
- Hu et al. (2021) set an 18M budget for GPT-3 175B — 0.01% of total parameters.
- That budget allows: one matrix at rank 8, two at rank 4, or all four at rank 2.

---

## Applying LoRA via Matrix Decomposition

- To apply LoRA to a weight matrix W, decompose it into the product of two matrices A and B.
- The rank of each factorization is a key configuration decision.
- Keeping LoRA adapters separate allows reuse of the same full-rank matrix W in multi-task settings.

---

## LoRA: Which Matrices to Adapt
- GPT-3 175B: 96 layers, dimension 12,288; rank-2 LoRA on four matrices gives ~18.9M trainable parameters
- Applying LoRA to all four matrices with rank 2 performed best on WikiSQL and MultiNLI
- With only two attention matrices, query and value matrices generally yield the best results
- More matrices, including feedforward, tend to improve results further

---

## Feedforward and Attention LoRA
- Databricks reported its biggest performance boost came from applying LoRA to all feedforward layers
- Feedforward-based LoRA can be complementary to attention-based LoRA
- Attention-based LoRA typically offers greater efficacy within memory constraints
- A small rank, such as between 4 and 64, is usually sufficient for many use cases

---

## Rank and Alpha Hyperparameters
- Increasing r did not increase finetuning performance in the LoRA authors' observations
- Databricks: increasing r beyond a certain value may not improve output quality; higher r may overfit
- Raschka found r = 256 achieved the best performance on his tasks
- α controls the W_AB contribution; α:r ratio typically between 1:8 and 8:1, requiring experimentation

---

## Serving LoRA Adapters: Two Options

- Option 1: merge LoRA weights A and B into W to create Wʹ before serving.
- Merging before serving adds no extra inference computation or latency.
- Option 2: keep W, A, and B separate; merging happens during inference, adding latency.
- Option 1 suits a single LoRA model; option 2 suits multi-LoRA serving.

---

## Multi-LoRA Serving Benefits

- Keeping adapters separate reuses one full-rank W across many LoRA models.
- Example: W is 4096×4096 (16.8M params); rank 8 gives A and B = 65,536 params.
- Option 1 with 100 customers: 16.8M × 100 = 1.68B parameters stored.
- Option 2: 16.8M + 65,536 × 100 = 23.3M parameters stored.

---

## Switching, Reuse, and Drawbacks

- Option 2 speeds task switching: load only the customer's LoRA adapter, not full weights.
- Apple used multiple LoRA adapters on one 3B base model for iPhone features (2024).
- Adapters are shareable and reusable, available on Hugging Face and AdapterHub.
- Drawback: LoRA performs below full finetuning and requires modifying model implementation.

---

## Quantized LoRA: Motivation

- LoRA adapter memory is minimal compared to the model’s weights.
- Reducing LoRA parameters cuts overall memory footprint only slightly.
- More effective: quantize the model’s weights, activations, and/or gradients.
- QLoRA is an early promising quantized LoRA variant (Dettmers et al., 2023).

---

## How QLoRA Works

- Original LoRA stores model weights in 16 bits during finetuning.
- QLoRA stores weights in 4 bits, dequantizing to BF16 for forward and backward passes.
- Uses NF4 (NormalFloat-4), which quantizes based on weights’ normal distribution around zero.
- Paged optimizers transfer data between CPU and GPU when GPU memory runs out.

---

## QLoRA Results and Limitations

- Techniques allow a 65B-parameter model to be finetuned on a single 48 GB GPU.
- Guanaco models (Llama 7B–65B, 4-bit) were competitive on benchmarks and comparative evaluation.
- Guanaco 65B did not outperform GPT-4 but was often preferred to ChatGPT.
- Limitation: NF4 quantization is expensive and may increase training time.

---

## Model Merging: Definition
- Finetuning alters a single model; model merging combines multiple models into a custom model.
- Merging offers greater flexibility: merge available models, or finetune constituents before merging.
- The merged model can be used without further finetuning, though finetuning often improves performance.
- Without finetuning, model merging can be done without GPUs, attractive to indie developers with limited compute.

---

## Goals and Added Value
- Goal: create one model that provides more value than using constituent models separately.
- Added value can be improved performance: merge two models good at different things on the same task.
- Example: one model answers first 60% of questions, another last 60%; combined perhaps 80%.
- Added value can be reduced memory footprint and costs, especially for adapter-based models.

---

## Multi-Task Finetuning and Adapters
- Model merging is important for multi-task finetuning.
- Without merging, finetuning a model for multiple tasks generally requires one of several approaches.
- For two models finetuned on the same base model, their adapters can be combined into a single adapter.
- This adapter combination is particularly attractive for reducing parameters while supporting both tasks.

---

## Simultaneous Finetuning
- Create a dataset containing examples for all the tasks
- Finetune the model on this dataset
- Goal: make the model learn all tasks simultaneously
- One model trained across multiple tasks at once

---

## Tradeoffs of Simultaneous Finetuning
- Learning multiple skills at the same time is generally harder
- This approach typically requires more data
- It also typically requires more training
- Simultaneous learning raises data and compute demands

---

## Sequential Finetuning and Catastrophic Forgetting

- Sequential finetuning trains the model on task A, then task B, and so on.
- Assumption: it is easier for models to learn one task at a time.
- Neural networks are prone to catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016).
- A model can forget an old task when trained on a new one, hurting earlier performance.

---

## Model Merging for Multi-Task Finetuning

- Finetune separately on each task in parallel, then merge the resulting models.
- Separate finetuning lets the model learn each task better; no sequential learning means less forgetting.
- Merging suits on-device deployment, where memory is limited and data may not leave the device.
- Model merging is one way to do federated learning (McMahan et al., 2016).

---

## Ensembling vs. Merging, and Merging Approaches

- Ensembling combines only model outputs, keeping each constituent model intact.
- Ensembling can improve performance but costs more inference calls per request.
- Merging typically mixes constituent model parameters; many top leaderboard models are merged.
- Three approaches: summing, layer stacking, and concatenation; these can be mixed.

---

## Summing Models
- Summing merges models by adding their weight values together.
- Two summing methods: linear combination and spherical linear interpolation (SLERP).
- 

---

## Rescaling Before Summing
- Models may have parameters in different scales.
- One model’s parameter values can be much larger than the other’s.
- Rescale models before summing so parameter values are in the same range.

---

## Linear Combination Basics
- Linear combination merges models via average or weighted average: Merge(A,B)=wA A + wB B.
- Works surprisingly well; studied since early 1990s; often used in federated learning.
- Model soups average entire weights of multiple finetuned models, improving accuracy without extra inference time.
- More common to merge specific components like adapters.

---

## Task Vectors and Task Arithmetic
- Linear combination most effective for models finetuned on same base model.
- Task vector = finetuned model minus base model; captures task essence; also called delta parameters.
- LoRA finetuning allows constructing task vector from LoRA weights.
- Task arithmetic: add task vectors to combine capabilities; subtract to reduce capabilities (e.g., remove biases).

---

## Alignment and Flexibility
- Linear combination works for same architecture/size; can project layers to same dimension for different sizes.
- Aligning models before averaging ensures functionally related parameters are averaged together.
- Alignment methods include optimal transport, Git Re-Basin, matching in task parameter subspaces.
- Alignment is challenging, so less common than naive linear combinations.

---

## SLERP for Model Summing
- SLERP is another common model summing method.
- It is based on the mathematical operator Spherical LinEar inteRPolation.
- The operator’s name is abbreviated as SLERP.

---

## Model Merging as Interpolation
- Interpolation estimates unknown values from known values.
- In model merging, the unknown is the merged model.
- Known values are the constituent models.
- Linear combination is one interpolation technique; SLERP is another.

---

## How SLERP Works
- Think of each vector to merge as a point on a sphere.
- Draw the shortest path between the two points along the surface.
- The merged vector lies somewhere along that shortest path.
- The interpolation factor, between 0 and 1, determines where.

---

## Interpolation Factor and Sequential Merging
- Factors below 0.5 bring the merged vector closer to the first vector.
- A factor of 0.5 picks the point exactly halfway.
- SLERP is defined with only two vectors, so it merges two at a time.
- To merge more than two, SLERP can be applied sequentially.

---

## Pruning Redundant Task-Specific Parameters
- Finetuning adjusts many parameters, but most adjustments are minor and redundant.
- Redundant adjustments may not hurt a single model but can harm a merged model.
- Yadav et al. (2023) showed resetting many task vector parameters causes minimal degradation.
- Resetting sets finetuned parameters back to their base-model values, zeroing task vector parameters.

---

## Keeping Only the Top Task Vector Parameters
- Keeping the top 20% of task vector parameters gave comparable performance to keeping 100%.
- TIES (Yadav et al., 2023) and DARE (Yu et al., 2023) prune redundant parameters before merging.
- Both papers showed pruning can significantly improve final merged model quality.
- Pruning becomes more important with more models, as redundant parameters can interfere across tasks.

---

## Layer Stacking (Frankenmerging)
- Take different layers from one or more models and stack them on top of each other.
- Also called *passthrough* or *frankenmerging*; can yield unique architectures and parameter counts.
- Unlike merging by summing, layer-stacked models typically need further finetuning for good performance.
- Early success: Goliath-120B merged two finetuned Llama 2-70B models, Xwin and Euryale, taking 72 of 80 layers from each.

---

## Building Mixture-of-Experts via Layer Stacking
- Per Komatsuzaki et al. (2022), "Sparse Upcycling" builds MoE models from dense checkpoints instead of training from scratch.
- Copy certain layers or modules of a pre-trained model, then add a router sending each input to the best copy.
- Further train the merged model with the router to refine performance.
- Layer stacking can outperform MoE models trained from scratch; Together AI's Mixture-of-Agents matched GPT-4o on some benchmarks.

---

## Model Upscaling with Depthwise Scaling
- Model upscaling: creating larger models using fewer resources than training from scratch.
- Depthwise scaling (Kim et al., 2023) built SOLAR 10.7B from one 7B model with 32 layers.
- Procedure: copy the pre-trained model, then sum certain layers and stack the rest.
- For SOLAR 10.7B, 16 layers were summed, giving 32 × 2 - 16 = 48 layers; then further train toward target performance.

## Concatenation in Model Merging
- Instead of adding parameters, concatenate the constituent models’ parameters.
- The merged component’s parameter count is the sum of all constituent components.
- Merging two LoRA adapters of ranks r1 and r2 yields rank r1 + r2.

---

## Trade-offs of Concatenation
- Concatenation does not reduce memory footprint compared with serving models separately.
- It might give better performance than separate models.
- The incremental performance might not justify the extra parameters.
- Concatenation is therefore not recommended.

---

## Base Models & Development Paths
- Start with the most powerful model you can afford; weaker models likely perform worse.
- Progression path: test code with cheapest model, data with middling model, then best model.
- Progression path ends by training all models to map price/performance frontier.
- Distillation path: train strong model on small data, generate more data, train cheaper model.

---

## Finetuning Methods & Frameworks
- LoRA is cost-effective but typically delivers less performance than full finetuning.
- Full finetuning needs thousands of examples; PEFT can work with a few hundred.
- LoRA efficiently serves multiple models sharing a base model with a single full model.
- Frameworks like LLaMA-Factory, unsloth, PEFT, Axolotl, LitGPT; distributed training via DeepSpeed, PyTorch Distributed, ColossalAI.

---

## Finetuning Hyperparameters
- Learning rate is step size; experiment between 1e-7 and 1e-3; loss curve hints.
- Batch size too small (<8) causes unstable training; larger batch needs more memory.
- Gradient accumulation updates weights after accumulating gradients across several batches.
- Small datasets may need more epochs; millions of examples need 1–2 epochs.

---

## Prompt Loss Weight
- Instruction finetuning examples contain prompts and responses; both can contribute to training loss.
- At inference, users provide prompts; the model only needs to generate responses.
- Response tokens should contribute more to loss than prompt tokens during training.
- Prompt loss weight: 100% equal, 0% responses only, typically 10% default.

---

## Finetuning Context
- Finetuning process is not hard; frameworks handle training and suggest sensible default hyperparameters.
- Context is complex: whether to finetune, reasons for/against, and when to use RAG.
- Early finetuning updated entire weights, similar to pre-training.
- Larger models made full finetuning impractical; more parameters require more memory.

---

## Memory, LoRA, Merging, Data
- PEFT reduces memory by reducing trainable parameters; quantized training reduces bits per value.
- LoRA is parameter-efficient, data-efficient, and modular, easing serving and combining multiple LoRA models.
- Model merging combines models into one better model; use cases include on-device deployment and upscaling.
- High-quality annotated instruction data is challenging; the next chapter covers obtaining it.
