# Task 2.1 - Choose appropriate modeling approaches for ML and AI solutions

## Overview

Task 2.1, Choose appropriate modeling approaches for ML and AI solutions, is the decision that constrains every decision after it. It asks whether a problem needs a trained model at all, and if it does, whether the answer lives in an organization's own data or in a capability a foundation model already carries. 

Interpretability, latency, and cost belong inside that decision rather than after it.

As you review these lessons for Task 2.1, check that you understand how to do the following.

- Evaluate and select appropriate FMs from Amazon Bedrock based on task requirements and performance criteria.
- Identify fine-tuning strategies for pre-trained FMs to meet business needs.
- Compare and select appropriate ML models, generative AI (GenAI) models, algorithms, and solution templates to meet business needs (for example, interpretability, domain-specific performance, latency).
- Evaluate tradeoffs between custom solutions, managed services, pre-trained models, and FMs to meet business needs.
- Select Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) architecture patterns based on use case requirements.
- Assess tradeoffs between ML model performance, training time, and cost.
- Assess tradeoffs between AI model performance, latency, and cost.
- Apply AWS AI services to solve specific business problems (for example, Amazon Textract, Amazon Rekognition, Amazon Comprehend, Amazon Transcribe).

## AWS services overview

The services in this task divide by where the capability comes from: a foundation model reached through one API, a managed service that already performs a specific task, or a training service that fits a model to an organization's own data. Recognize what each one removes from the build. Review each service to learn more.

### Selecting foundation models and fine-tuning strategies

In practice, this group of skills lets the machine learning engineer pick a foundation model on evidence rather than reputation, and then decide whether that model needs customizing at all.

Be comfortable doing the following:

- Comparing available foundation models against what the task actually requires, including output quality, response latency, and cost per request, and treating the comparison as a measurement rather than an opinion.
- Deciding when a fine-tuning strategy is warranted, and which one: supervised fine-tuning from labeled examples, reinforcement fine-tuning from reward functions that score responses, or distillation of a larger model's behavior into a smaller one.
- Recognizing what each customization path asks you to prepare, because distillation is prepared as a set of prompts and the managed path generates the teacher responses from them, while supervised fine-tuning is prepared as labeled input and output pairs.

The order of operations is where teams lose time. Task-specific prompt engineering, with clear instructions and a few worked examples, usually closes a wording or format gap on its own, and it changes immediately. Customization asks for prepared data, a training job, and a version to manage, so it earns its place only when evaluations show a gap that prompting cannot close. Customization also carries a real risk worth naming: a model trained hard on a narrow domain can lose general capability it previously had, which is catastrophic forgetting, and blending broader data into the training mixture is the usual defense.

### Knowledge Check

Evaluations show a foundation model still misses an organization's internal terminology after prompt instructions and worked examples were added. Which step is the MOST appropriate next one? *(Choose 1)*

* **A.** Fine-tune the model on labeled examples that demonstrate the expected wording
* **B.** Add more retrieved passages to the prompt at request time
* **C.** Raise the limit on how many tokens the model may generate
* **D.** Select a smaller model to reduce inference cost

??? success "Answer & Explanation"

    **Correct Answer:** **A. Fine-tune the model on labeled examples that demonstrate the expected wording**

    **Explanation:**  
    The standard progression for adapting foundation model behavior is: **Prompt Engineering (Instructions + Few-Shot Examples) $\longrightarrow$ Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**. When prompt engineering and in-context learning fail to consistently enforce domain-specific terminology, style, or formatting guidelines, fine-tuning the model on a dataset of labeled prompt-response pairs (`JSONL`) updates the model's internal weights to consistently adopt the target vocabulary.

    **Why the other options are incorrect:**
    * **B. Add more retrieved passages to the prompt at request time:** RAG supplies external factual knowledge at query time, but simply stuffing more context into the prompt window does not teach the model how to adhere to specific terminology or stylistic conventions if few-shot examples have already failed.
    * **C. Raise the limit on how many tokens the model may generate:** Increasing `max_tokens` allows the model to produce longer outputs, but does not improve the semantic precision or terminology of the generated text.
    * **D. Select a smaller model to reduce inference cost:** Switching to a smaller foundation model generally reduces reasoning capabilities and instruction-following quality, making it even less likely to follow specialized internal terminology.

### Weighing custom builds, managed services, and pre-trained models
In practice, this group of skills lets the machine learning engineer say no to building something, which is often the highest-value decision available.

Be comfortable doing the following:

- Comparing candidate models, algorithms, and solution templates against the requirements that are easy to forget: interpretability for the people who must defend a prediction, performance on this specific domain, and the latency the application can absorb.
- Weighing a custom build against a managed service, a pre-trained model, and a foundation model, and being explicit about what each option costs in engineering time as well as in compute.
- Reaching for the AWS AI service that already performs a solved task, such as extracting content from documents, analyzing images and video, finding structure in text, or transcribing speech.

Two failure modes bracket this decision. One is building a model for a capability a managed service already provides, which buys nothing and creates something to maintain. The other is reaching for a model when the logic is fixed, where a plain rule is cheaper, faster, and easier to audit. Between those, the question is where the answer lives. Patterns that exist only inside an organization's own history call for training on that history. A general capability such as summarizing language already exists inside a foundation model, and no amount of local history teaches a regression model to write a sentence.

### Knowledge Check

A team must pull text out of scanned invoices, and no comparable model exists in house. Which approach reaches a working result with the LEAST development effort? *(Choose 1)*

* **A.** Call a managed AWS AI service that already extracts text from documents
* **B.** Train a custom computer vision model on labeled invoice images
* **C.** Fine-tune a foundation model on a corpus of past invoices
* **D.** Write fixed rules that read text at set positions on each layout

??? success "Answer & Explanation"

    **Correct Answer:** **A. Call a managed AWS AI service that already extracts text from documents**

    **Explanation:**  
    AWS managed AI services like **Amazon Textract** (specifically the `AnalyzeExpense` API) are pre-trained on millions of documents, forms, and invoices. Calling a managed API requires zero custom model training, zero manual labeling, and no dedicated compute infrastructure to manage—making it the option with the lowest development effort and fastest time-to-market.

    **Why the other options are incorrect:**
    * **B. Train a custom computer vision model on labeled invoice images:** Requires building a bounding-box annotation workflow, selecting vision/OCR architectures, training, and managing hosting infrastructure, incurring high development and operational overhead.
    * **C. Fine-tune a foundation model on a corpus of past invoices:** Preparing specialized multimodal datasets and running fine-tuning jobs is resource-intensive and unnecessary for standard document key-value extraction.
    * **D. Write fixed rules that read text at set positions on each layout:** Position-based bounding box rules are extremely brittle. Invoices vary widely across vendors, causing coordinate-based rules to fail whenever layouts, margins, or scan scales change.

### Retrieval patterns and the performance, latency, and cost tradeoffs

In practice, this group of skills lets the machine learning engineer read a set of requirements and derive the architecture from them, instead of choosing an architecture and hoping the requirements fit.

Be comfortable doing the following:

- Selecting a retrieval pattern from the use case, and knowing the parts a production retrieval system needs whichever pattern you choose: connectors to the sources, document processing, an embedding model, a vector store, a retriever, the generating model, guardrails, and an orchestrator.
- Choosing between a fully managed retrieval service and a custom retrieval architecture, where the managed path removes work and the custom path returns control over components such as the retriever and the vector store.
- Assessing tradeoffs between model performance, training time, and cost for a trained model, and between output quality, latency, and cost for a foundation model, and naming which one a requirement is asking you to give up.

Retrieval and customization answer different questions, so the requirements pick between them. Retrieval keeps content outside the model, which means an edited document is reflected as soon as it is re-ingested, and the response can point at the source it used. Customization writes behavior into the weights, so a content change waits for another training job, and there is no source to cite. Retrieval is a weaker fit when a request needs whole-document synthesis rather than the retrieval of relevant passages, and the two approaches can be combined when the requirements genuinely call for both.

### Knowledge Check

A support assistant must cite the document behind each answer and must reflect a policy edit within minutes. Which property of a retrieval approach satisfies both requirements? *(Choose 1)*

* **A.** The answer is generated from passages retrieved at request time
* **B.** The policy text sits in the model weights after a customization job
* **C.** A larger model is selected to raise answer quality
* **D.** Requests are batched to lower the cost of each response

??? success "Answer & Explanation"

    **Correct Answer:** **A. The answer is generated from passages retrieved at request time**

    **Explanation:**  
    Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) fetches fresh, relevant passages from a vector database at query time and injects them directly into the foundation model's prompt context. Because retrieval relies on an external vector index rather than model weights:
    1. **Document Citations:** Each retrieved chunk carries source metadata (such as document title, section, or page number) that the model can explicitly cite in its final answer.
    2. **Near Real-Time Updates:** When a policy changes, updating or re-embedding the affected document chunk in the vector index takes only minutes, immediately providing up-to-date knowledge without requiring model fine-tuning or re-training.

    **Why the other options are incorrect:**
    * **B. The policy text sits in the model weights after a customization job:** Customizing model weights via fine-tuning takes hours or days, cannot easily cite exact source documents, and requires continuous, costly re-training whenever policies are edited.
    * **C. A larger model is selected to raise answer quality:** Model parameters affect reasoning capability, but a larger model still cannot retrieve newly updated document edits or provide verified source citations unless paired with a retrieval mechanism.
    * **D. Requests are batched to lower the cost of each response:** Batching requests optimizes compute throughput and inference costs, but has no effect on knowledge freshness or document attribution.

> **Core Principle:** Model selection is not just about listing options—it is about matching business constraints (interpretability, latency, cost) with where the underlying knowledge or capability resides.

### 1. The 4-Tier Selection Hierarchy

| Problem Profile | Recommended Approach | Example AWS Tool / Implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fixed Business Logic** | **Simple Rules / Code** | Plain code conditionals (e.g., reorder stock if $qty < 10$) |
| **Pre-Defined / Standard Task** | **Managed AWS AI Service** | Amazon Textract, Rekognition, Transcribe, Comprehend |
| **Patterns in Proprietary Data** | **Train Traditional ML Model** | Amazon SageMaker AI built-in algorithms (e.g., XGBoost) |
| **General Language / Creative Task** | **Foundation Model (Bedrock)** | Amazon Bedrock + Prompting $\longrightarrow$ Fine-Tuning (if needed) |

### 2. Comparative Scenario Analysis

#### Scenario A: Weekly Demand Forecasting (Traditional ML)

* **Goal:** Predict store-level sales from historical transactions for finance stakeholders.
* **Core Insight:** *"The answer is already in your data."*
* **Approach:** Supervised Regression using historical sales, seasonality, and promotional features via **Amazon SageMaker AI**.
* **Key Constraint:** **Interpretability & Stakeholder Trust.** A simpler, explainable model that finance trusts and understands always beats a complex "black box" model that gets rejected.

#### Scenario B: Support Ticket Summarization (Generative AI)

* **Goal:** Generate concise summaries of long support tickets using company terminology.
* **Core Insight:** *"The capability is already inside the model."*
* **Approach:** Managed Foundation Model via **Amazon Bedrock**.
* **Key Constraint:** **Deployment Speed & Cost.** Summarizing language is a general capability. No amount of tabular history teaches a model to write sentences.

### 3. Golden Architectural Rules for the Exam

#### Rule 1: The "Prompt First, Fine-Tune Second" Order

```
          ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
          │   Task-Specific Prompt Engineering     │
          │   (Instructions + Few-Shot Examples)    │
          └────────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                               │
                               ▼
                    Run Performance Evaluation
                               │
            ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
            ▼                                     ▼
     Meets Quality Bar?                    Fails Consistently?
            │                                     │
            ▼                                     ▼
     Deploy to Prod                     Consider Fine-Tuning
     (Zero Training Overhead)           (Prepped Data, GPU Cost,
                                         Version Control Overhead)

```

* **Always start with Prompt Engineering:** Task-specific instructions and few-shot examples inside a prompt usually cover specialized terminology and formatting.
* **Fine-tune only as a last resort:** Fine-tuning incurs significant overhead (dataset preparation, continuous training costs, model versioning). Only accept this overhead when evaluations prove prompting alone cannot bridge the gap.

#### Rule 2: Non-Functional Constraints Shape Model Choice

Model selection is rarely driven by raw accuracy alone. The primary evaluation criteria are:

1. **Interpretability:** Can business stakeholders understand and audit how predictions are made?
2. **Inference Latency:** Does the model meet real-time or batch response SLAs?
3. **Cost & Overhead:** What is the long-term operational cost of hosting, maintaining, and updating the model?

A retail company needs weekly demand forecasts drawn from years of its own sales history, and the finance team must be able to explain each prediction. Which approach fits those requirements BEST? *(Choose 1)*

* **A.** Call a managed AWS AI service that generates written commentary about demand
* **B.** Train a supervised model on the company's history and favor an interpretable algorithm
* **C.** Select a large foundation model and supply the sales history in the prompt
* **D.** Write fixed reorder rules that trigger at a stock threshold

??? success "Answer & Explanation"

    **Correct Answer:** **B. Train a supervised model on the company's history and favor an interpretable algorithm**

    **Explanation:**  
    When historical transactional data exists within an organization and contains complex trends/seasonality, **training a supervised ML model** (e.g., a regression algorithm) on proprietary data is the optimal path. Furthermore, because finance stakeholders require explainability, choosing an interpretable algorithm (or pairing a tree-based model with SHAP/SageMaker Clarify feature attribution) satisfies the core business constraint without using a "black box" model.

    **Why the other options are incorrect:**
    * **A. Call a managed AWS AI service that generates written commentary about demand:** Higher-level AI services or written commentary do not perform store-level numerical time-series forecasting tailored to proprietary historical sales tables.
    * **C. Select a large foundation model and supply the sales history in the prompt:** LLMs and foundation models are not designed for precise, large-scale tabular time-series demand forecasting across thousands of SKUs and store locations, and context window limits make passing years of raw tabular data inefficient and costly.
    * **D. Write fixed reorder rules that trigger at a stock threshold:** Static inventory rules cannot model dynamic demand patterns, seasonality, promotional impacts, or regional trends.
